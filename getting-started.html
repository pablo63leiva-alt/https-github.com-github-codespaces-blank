Getting Started in the Trades

Breaking into the trades as a teen or young adult doesn't have to be complicated. Whether you're still in high school or just graduated, this step-by-step guide will show you exactly how to build a rewarding career in the skilled trades — with real money, zero student debt, and skills that last a lifetime.

Why Start Now?

The best time to start building trade skills is today. Here's why getting into the trades early gives you a massive advantage.

⏰

Head Start

While your classmates rack up $40,000+ in student loan debt, you'll already be earning a paycheck and gaining real-world experience. By the time they graduate, you'll be a skilled professional with years on the job.

📈

Growing Demand

The skilled trades face a massive labor shortage with over 750,000 unfilled positions nationwide. Companies are desperate for new talent and are willing to pay top dollar to attract and retain skilled workers.

💪

Real Skills

Trade skills are recession-proof and transferable. Whether you work for a company or start your own business, the abilities you develop will be valuable for your entire life. You can't outsource plumbing or welding.

Your 5-Step Roadmap to a Trade Career

Follow these five steps to go from zero experience to a working tradesperson. Each step builds on the last, so take your time and do it right.

1

Pick Your Trade

The first and most important step is figuring out which trade actually interests you. There's no single "best" trade — the right one depends on your personality, interests, and long-term goals. Start by asking yourself some honest questions: Do you prefer working with your hands solving physical problems, or do you enjoy diagnostics and technical troubleshooting? Do you want to work outdoors year-round, or would you rather have a climate-controlled workspace? Do you prefer working independently on your own projects, or do you thrive as part of a crew?

Some trades lean more toward physical, outdoor work — like ironworking, carpentry, or concrete finishing. Others are more technical and precision-oriented, like electrical work, HVAC, or instrumentation. And some, like welding, offer a mix of both. Consider what excites you most. If you've ever taken apart a bicycle just to see how it works, mechanical trades might be your thing. If you like building things with wood, carpentry could be your calling. The key is to explore before you commit.

Visit job sites if you can. Talk to people already working in different trades. Watch YouTube channels dedicated to specific trades — seeing a day in the life of an electrician versus a plumber can be incredibly revealing. Many trade unions and community colleges also offer free career exploration events where you can try out different trades hands-on. Don't rush this step. Spending a few weeks researching now can save you years of being in the wrong career.

Pro Tip: Check out our Trades page for detailed breakdowns of 10+ different trades, including salary ranges, training timelines, and what a typical day looks like for each.
2

Get Educated

Once you've narrowed down your interests, it's time to start learning. The good news is that you don't need a four-year degree to get into the trades. There are several education pathways, and many of them are free or low-cost. Trade schools (also called vocational or technical schools) offer focused programs that teach you the specific skills you need for your chosen trade in 6 months to 2 years. Community colleges often have excellent trade programs at a fraction of the cost of private trade schools, and many offer financial aid or scholarships specifically for trade students.

Union apprenticeships are another fantastic option and arguably the gold standard for trade education. In an apprenticeship, you work alongside experienced journeymen while attending classes, learning the trade from the inside out. Most union apprenticeships are sponsored by employers, meaning they pay for your training while you earn a wage. It's truly earn-while-you-learn. Programs typically last 3 to 5 years depending on the trade, and upon completion, you become a licensed journeyman with full earning potential.

Even if you're still in high school, there are things you can do right now. Many high schools offer vocational-technical (vo-tech) programs during the school day. Enroll in shop classes, drafting courses, or any hands-on electives available. Some schools partner with local trade unions to offer pre-apprenticeship programs for juniors and seniors. Contact your local union hall or trade association to ask about youth programs and summer opportunities. The earlier you start building foundational skills — even basic ones like how to read a tape measure, use hand tools safely, or understand basic math — the better positioned you'll be when you're ready to apply for formal training.

Pro Tip: Don't overlook community colleges. Many offer the same trade programs as expensive private trade schools at a fraction of the cost. Always compare tuition, job placement rates, and instructor credentials before enrolling anywhere.
3

Get Certified

Certifications are your ticket to proving you know your stuff. They show employers that you've met industry standards and take safety seriously. The first certification every aspiring tradesperson should earn is the OSHA 10-Hour certification. This is a general workplace safety course that covers hazard recognition, fall protection, electrical safety, and emergency procedures. It's required or strongly preferred for most entry-level construction and trade positions, and you can complete it in just two days. Many states now require OSHA 10 for anyone working on a construction site. You must be at least 16 years old to earn it, and it's available both online and in-person.

Beyond OSHA 10, focus on certifications specific to your chosen trade. For electricians, that might mean the Electrical Safety Certification or getting familiar with the National Electrical Code (NEC). For HVAC technicians, the EPA Section 608 certification for handling refrigerants is essential — it's legally required for anyone who purchases or handles refrigerants. Welders should look into the American Welding Society (AWS) certifications for their specific welding processes. Plumbers and pipefitters need to understand local plumbing codes and may need state or city-specific licenses.

First Aid and CPR certification is another smart move regardless of your trade. Job sites can be dangerous, and knowing how to respond to an emergency could save a life — including your own. Many employers value this certification highly, and it's a relatively quick and inexpensive course to complete. As you advance in your career, you'll want to stack additional certifications. OSHA 30 (the advanced safety course), confined space entry, rigging, forklift operation, and trade-specific journeyman licenses will all increase your value and earning potential over time. Think of certifications as investments in your future — each one opens new doors and justifies higher pay.

Pro Tip: Start with OSHA 10 right now — it's the single most universally valued credential for entry-level trade workers. Many training centers offer it for $25-$75 or even free through workforce development programs. Check with your local community college or union hall.
4

Build Experience

Experience is what separates you from every other applicant. You don't need to be a master craftsman to start — you just need to show that you've put in the time and effort to learn. The best way to build experience when you're starting from scratch is to start with part-time work, helper positions, or even volunteer opportunities. Many local contractors hire laborers and helpers with no experience. These roles won't make you rich, but they'll teach you the basics: how to read blueprints, use power tools safely, follow instructions from a foreman, and understand how a job site operates.

Volunteering is another powerful way to gain hands-on skills while giving back to your community. Habitat for Humanity is one of the best options — they welcome volunteers of all skill levels and teach you real construction skills from framing to finish work. You'll learn teamwork, proper tool usage, and construction fundamentals while helping families build their homes. Many Habitat chapters also offer skilled trade volunteer days specifically for people interested in electrical, plumbing, or HVAC work. It looks great on a resume and demonstrates initiative that employers love to see.

Don't underestimate the value of side work either. Offer to help neighbors with projects — fixing a fence, painting a room, repairing drywall, helping with a deck build. These small projects teach you problem-solving, time management, and client communication. You'll learn to work with different materials, use tools you might not have encountered otherwise, and develop a portfolio of completed work that you can show potential employers. Keep a log or take photos of your projects. Even if they're small, they show that you're proactive and passionate about the trade. If you're still in high school, ask if you can job shadow at a local trade shop for a day. Many business owners are happy to show a motivated young person the ropes — it costs them nothing and might earn them a future employee.

Pro Tip: Document everything you build or repair, even small projects. Take before-and-after photos. When you apply for apprenticeships or jobs, showing real work you've done speaks louder than any certificate or resume line.
5

Land Your First Job

With your education, certifications, and some experience under your belt, it's time to start applying. The trades offer multiple pathways into your first position. Apprenticeships through unions are one of the strongest options. Most union apprenticeship programs have specific application windows — check with your local union hall or visit apprenticeship.gov to find programs in your area. You'll typically need to pass a basic math and reading test, have a high school diploma or GED, and be at least 18 years old (16 for some programs with parental consent).

Non-union shops and private contractors are another great entry point. Many smaller contractors prefer to hire helpers and train them up themselves. Search job boards like Indeed, Craigslist, and local Facebook groups for terms like "helper," "laborer," "entry-level [trade name]," or "apprentice." Don't be afraid to walk into a local shop and ask if they're hiring — in the trades, showing up in person with a firm handshake and a good attitude still means something. Bring a copy of your certifications, any photos of work you've done, and references from volunteer work or side projects.

Your experience, certifications, and eagerness to learn are your leverage. Employers in the trades are much more interested in your attitude and work ethic than your GPA. They want someone who shows up on time, stays off their phone, asks smart questions, and isn't afraid to get dirty. Emphasize those qualities in every interaction. When you land that first position, show up early every single day, say yes to every task (even the ones nobody else wants), and never stop learning. The trades reward hard work and loyalty — put in the effort and your career will take off faster than you ever imagined.

Pro Tip: When applying for your first trade job, emphasize your certifications and attitude over experience. Most hiring managers know you're starting out — they're looking for someone who will show up, work hard, and stick around. Demonstrate those qualities and you'll stand out.

Apprenticeships: Earn While You Learn

Apprenticeships are the single most effective way to break into a trade. They combine on-the-job training with classroom instruction, all while paying you a wage from day one.

💰

What Are Apprenticeships?

An apprenticeship is a structured training program that combines hands-on work experience with related technical instruction. You work under the supervision of an experienced journeyman who teaches you the trade from the ground up, while also attending classroom sessions to learn the theory and safety behind your work. It's the most respected pathway into any trade because it's earned through real, on-the-job experience.

🔄

How They Work

Most apprenticeships follow a progressive wage model — you start at a percentage of a journeyman's pay (typically 40-50%) and earn raises as you complete each year of training. Your wages increase as your skills grow, meaning you're earning more every year while you learn. By the time you complete your apprenticeship, you'll be earning full journeyman wages, which are significantly higher than entry-level pay.

📅

Typical Duration

Most apprenticeships last between 3 and 5 years depending on the trade. Electrical and plumbing apprenticeships typically run 4-5 years, while welding or HVAC apprenticeships might be 2-3 years. During that time, you'll log a specific number of on-the-job training hours (usually 8,000 to 10,000) and complete a set number of classroom hours before you can test for your journeyman license.

🔍

How to Find Them

The best place to start is apprenticeship.gov, the official U.S. government website for registered apprenticeship programs. You can search by trade, state, and zip code to find programs near you. Local union halls are another excellent resource — each trade union maintains an apprenticeship program, and they often have open houses or information sessions. Contact your nearest IBEW (electricians), UA (plumbers/pipefitters), or Local union to learn about their application process and deadlines.

🏢

Union vs. Non-Union

Both union and non-union apprenticeships offer excellent training. Union programs are highly structured with standardized curricula and strong job placement rates. Non-union programs, often run by individual contractors or trade associations, may offer more flexibility and local job connections. Neither is inherently better — it depends on your local market, your personal preference, and which opportunities are available. The key is to choose a registered program with a solid reputation and good job placement numbers.

Essential Certifications to Get Started

While you don't need every certification under the sun to start, these credentials will immediately make you more competitive and open doors to better jobs.

🛡️

OSHA 10-Hour

Safety

The most universally required entry-level certification. This 10-hour course covers workplace hazard recognition, fall protection, electrical safety, and emergency response procedures. It's required or strongly preferred on nearly every construction site and manufacturing facility in the country. You can complete it online or in-person for $25-$100. You must be at least 16 years old. This is the single most important credential you can have as a new trade worker — get it first.

🏥

First Aid / CPR

Safety

Job sites are inherently dangerous environments where accidents can happen in a split second. Being certified in First Aid and CPR means you can respond effectively when someone gets hurt. Many employers consider this certification a must-have, especially for foremen and crew leads. The Red Cross and American Heart Association both offer affordable courses that can be completed in a single day. Knowing how to handle an emergency could literally save a coworker's life — and it's a credential that sets you apart from candidates without it.

⚡

Trade-Specific Certs

Trade-Specific

Each trade has its own certifications that prove your competency in that specialty. For HVAC technicians, the EPA Section 608 certification is legally required for anyone who purchases or handles refrigerants. For welders, AWS (American Welding Society) certifications for specific processes like MIG, TIG, and stick welding demonstrate your skill level to employers. Electricians need to understand the National Electrical Code (NEC). Plumbers and pipefitters may need state or city-specific licensing. Research which certifications are most valued in your local market and prioritize accordingly.

🚗

Driver's License

Essential

Don't overlook this one — a valid driver's license is required for a surprisingly large number of trade positions. Electricians, plumbers, HVAC techs, and many other tradespeople drive service vehicles to job sites daily. Even if you're not driving a company truck, you need reliable transportation to get to job sites that are often in different locations each day. Get your driver's license as soon as you're eligible. A clean driving record is an additional asset that many employers actively look for, especially for positions that involve company vehicles.

Tools to Buy First

Every tradesperson needs a basic toolkit to get started. You don't need to spend a fortune on day one — start with the essentials and build your collection over time as your skills and earnings grow.

🔧

Basic Hand Tools

Start with the fundamentals that every trade uses. A quality 16 oz hammer (not the cheapest one — invest in a good brand like Estwing), a 25-foot tape measure (Stanley FatMax is the industry standard), a set of channel-lock pliers, a set of flathead and Phillips screwdrivers, a utility knife, and a torpedo level. These tools will serve you across multiple trades and form the foundation of any toolkit. Buy the best you can afford — cheap tools break fast and end up costing more in the long run.

🦺

Safety Gear

Never skimp on safety equipment. A hard hat (Class E rated for electrical work), safety glasses with side shields (ANSI Z87.1 rated), steel-toe or composite-toe work boots (look for slip-resistant soles), and a good pair of work gloves are non-negotiable. Your eyes, head, hands, and feet are your most valuable assets on any job site. Many employers provide some safety gear, but it's smart to own your own backup set so you're always prepared.

🎒

Tool Bag / Belt

A good tool bag or belt keeps your essential tools within arm's reach and protects them from damage. A 16-inch or larger tool bag with multiple pockets works well for most trades. Leather tool belts are the traditional choice for construction trades, while a structured tool bag is better for service technicians who move between job sites. Look for something durable with reinforced stitching — you'll be loading it with heavy tools daily.

💵

Budget: $200-$500

You can get started with a solid basic toolkit for $200 to $500. Don't try to buy everything at once — start with the essentials and add tools as you need them and as your budget allows. Many experienced tradespeople recommend buying one quality tool at a time rather than a cheap complete set. Check for deals at Harbor Freight for budget-friendly options, or invest in mid-range brands like Klein Tools, DeWalt, or Milwaukee for tools you'll use every day. Some trade programs and unions also offer tool grants or starter kits to new apprentices.

Pro Tip: Never borrow tools without returning them immediately and in good condition. In the trades, your reputation for taking care of your equipment matters. And never, ever touch another person's tools without asking first — it's the cardinal rule of every job site.

5 Tips for Nailing Your Trade Job Interview

Landing the interview is half the battle. Here's how to make a strong impression and show employers you're the kind of person they want on their crew.

⏰

Show Up Early

Being late to a trade job interview is an instant disqualification in most hiring managers' eyes. In the trades, time is money — showing up late on day one means the entire crew is waiting for you. Aim to arrive 10-15 minutes early. This gives you time to find the office, calm your nerves, and show the employer that punctuality matters to you. Being early demonstrates respect for their time and shows that you take the opportunity seriously.

👔

Dress Clean but Ready to Work

You don't need a suit and tie for a trade interview — that might actually work against you. Instead, show up in clean, presentable work clothes: pressed jeans or work pants, a clean t-shirt or button-down, and clean work boots. This tells the interviewer that you understand the nature of the job and you're ready to get to work. Avoid ripped or dirty clothing, excessive cologne, or jewelry that could be a safety hazard on a job site.

🤝

Be Honest About Your Experience

Never exaggerate or lie about your experience level — tradespeople talk, and a bad reputation can follow you across an entire industry. If you're entry-level, own it. Say, "I'm just starting out, but I've completed my OSHA 10, I've been doing side work, and I'm eager to learn from experienced people." Honesty combined with demonstrated initiative is far more impressive than inflated claims that will fall apart on your first day. Most employers would rather train someone honest and motivated than deal with someone who misrepresented themselves.

📚

Show Willingness to Learn

The trades are all about continuous learning. Tell the interviewer that you want to learn everything you can, that you're not afraid of hard work, and that you're committed to getting better every day. Mention specific things you've done to prepare — your certifications, side projects, volunteer work, or classes you've taken. Employers invest heavily in training new tradespeople, so they need to know you're going to stick around and make that investment worth it.

❓

Ask Questions About the Company

Asking thoughtful questions shows genuine interest and separates you from candidates who just want any job. Ask about the types of projects they work on, what their training process looks like, whether they support employees getting additional certifications, and what the typical career progression looks like at their company. Questions like "What does a successful first year look like here?" or "What's the most important quality you look for in your crew?" demonstrate that you're thinking about how to succeed, not just how to get hired.
