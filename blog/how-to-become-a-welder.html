How to Become a Welder: The 5-Step Roadmap (2026)

Updated September 14, 2026 · 8 min read · Career Guide

Welding has the fastest on-ramp of any skilled trade: most people go from "never held a torch" to hirable with an AWS certification in about 6-7 months. Here's the honest 5-step roadmap — school vs apprenticeship, the certifications that actually get you hired, and the real cost-versus-income math.

Search "how to become a welder" and you'll get three versions of the same story: the trade schools that make it look like one semester to six figures, the apprenticeship advocates who say school is a waste, and the forums full of welders arguing about both. The truth is simpler and better: welding is one of the shortest, cheapest routes from "no experience" to "paid, certified, and hired" of any career in the country — if you follow the right steps.

Here's the roadmap, upfront: the fastest path to a real welding paycheck runs about 6-7 months — a community college certificate or trade school program that ends in an AWS certification, or a paid union apprenticeship that starts paying you from day one. Entry-level welders earn about $16-$20/hr, experienced welders land in the $45K-$70K range (BLS median roughly $47,000), and specialized pipe and TIG welders push past $80K-$110K+. This guide walks you through the five steps to the first paycheck — and the trade-offs at each one.

The Two Paths to the Hood: School vs Apprenticeship

Before the steps, the fork in the road: you can pay for a fast certificate, or get paid through an apprenticeship. Both end with you employed and certified — the difference is speed, cost, and how deep the specialization goes.

  Community College / Trade School Union Apprenticeship
Cost $2K-$15K (community college certificate $2K-$6K; trade school $5K-$15K) $0 — you get paid while you train
Time to hired 6-12 months to a certification and a production or shop job Hired as an apprentice immediately; 3-4 years to journey-level
Earning while training No — you pay tuition; most students work part-time Yes — entry welding apprentices earn $16-$20/hr with step raises
Where it leads Fastest entry; stack AWS certs (D1.1, 6G) to chase pipe and industrial work Deepest route to pipe/industrial/union scale, pensions, and $80K+ journey pay

Cost and timelines are realistic 2026 estimates, not guarantees. Both paths put you in the $45K-$70K experienced-welder range; the union track adds benefits and a pension, the school track gets you earning months sooner. The upfront cost of a degree you don't need — the $100K+ four-year route — is covered in our trade school vs college guide.

Step 1: Meet the Bare Minimum (Almost Anyone Does)

There is no license exam guarding the door to welding. The entry requirements are thin: a high school diploma or GED, the physical ability to stand and work in gear, and basic math (fractions, decimals, measuring — that's it). Most employers and union halls don't require prior experience for an apprenticeship; they test potential, not skill. When you apply, some programs run a small aptitude or hands-on screening, but preparation is easy — our Getting Started roadmap covers exactly how to prep for the interview and the test.

Step 2: Choose School or Apprenticeship

This is the fork from the table above, and the decision is mostly about your money and timeline:

  • Pick community college or trade school if you want the fastest route to a job and can cover $2K-$15K in tuition. For reference, that's a fraction of the average four-year degree — most welding grads have their training paid back within their first year.
  • Pick a union apprenticeship (through the United Association, Ironworkers, or local pipe/boilermaker halls) if you want $0 tuition, a paycheck from week one, and a structured 3-4 year climb to journey-level pay on union scale.

Whichever you choose, make sure the program is AWS-recognized and teaches the processes below. If you're under 18 and wondering which path to start, the timing breakdown in our best trades for 16-year-olds guide maps it out.

Step 3: Learn the Four Core Processes

Every welder's skill set is built on the same processes, and each one pays differently. You'll learn all of them; you'll specialize in two:

  • SMAW (stick): the workhorse for structural steel and field work — your foundation.
  • GMAW (MIG): fast, clean, and everywhere in fabrication shops; the typical first job.
  • FCAW (flux-cored): heavy structural and outdoor work where wind kills shielding gas.
  • GTAW (TIG): the precision process — thin metal, stainless, and pipe. TIG certification is one of the biggest pay levers in welding.

Most certificate programs run 6-12 months and log hundreds of hours in the booth across these four. That booth time is the part you can't skip — hiring managers don't care about the diploma, they care that you can pass a weld test on their terms.

Step 4: Pass the AWS Certifications That Get You Hired

Your first certification is the moment you're hireable. The American Welding Society (AWS) writes the tests employers actually respect:

  • AWS D1.1 (Structural): the entry-level money-maker — qualifying you to weld structural steel for buildings and bridges. This is the cert that lands first jobs.
  • Position certifications (fillet, groove, overhead): the more positions you qualify for, the more jobs you can take.
  • API 1104: the pipeline code — required for pipeline and utility work, and it pays a premium.
  • 6G pipe (SMAW/GTAW combo): the top-tier credential — a proven 6G welder is the first call for plant and pipeline contractors, and the gateway to $80K-$110K+.

Certifications must be renewed on a schedule, which sounds annoying and is actually good news: it means certified welders stay in demand and have a built-in reason to re-qualify and raise their rate. The certification roadmap by trade is on our Resources page.

Step 5: Get Hired — Then Stack Certs to Raise Your Rate

Your first job after certification is usually production or shop work: $16-$20/hr (roughly $33K-$40K/year). From there the salary ladder is honest and steep:

  • Entry-level shop (MIG): $17-$22/hr while you build speed and quality.
  • Experienced general welder: $45K-$70K with 2-5 years across fabrication and repair.
  • Pipe welder (6G / combo certified): $80K-$110K+ with travel and per-diem on top.
  • Underwater / commercial diver welder: $100K-$200K+ — the extreme top of the board, requiring diving credentials on top of welding.

The pattern to memorize: baseline welding pays fine; certified, specialized welding pays great. Most welders never chase the six-figure extremes and still land comfortably in the experienced range — which is why welding sits high on our highest paying jobs without a degree list.

Union vs Non-Union: Which Way to Work?

Sealing the deal on your first shop or hall changes what your certify-and-climb ladder looks like:

  • Union: paid apprenticeships, step-raises on a published scale, health coverage, and pensions. The catch: application windows, waitlists, and you work where the hall sends you.
  • Non-union: faster to start, freedom to chase jobs and specialties, and you negotiate your own rate once you're valuable. The catch: you also buy your own benefits, and pay swings more with the local economy.

Both paths produce welders making $60K+. The union route generally wins for stability and benefits; the non-union route wins for speed and independence. The deeper breakdown of this trade-off — and whether welding is the right career at all — lives in our is welding a good career review.

Job Outlook: The Shortage Works for You

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 2% growth for welders through 2033, with roughly 46,000 openings each year — and the openings number is the one that matters. A large share of the current workforce is retiring out of the trade faster than it's being replaced, and every energy construction boom (wind, solar, battery plants) needs welders on site. Robots have taken the most repetitive factory joints; everything custom, structural, and field-based still needs a person with a hood and a torch. That dynamic — not the modest growth rate — is what keeps certified welders in demand.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I become a welder with no experience?

Start an AWS-recognized training program — community college ($2K-$6K) or trade school ($5K-$15K) — or a paid union apprenticeship. Within about 6-7 months you can pass an entry-level AWS certification like D1.1 structural and get hired. No prior experience or degree is required to apply.

Do welders need a license or certification?

Most welding jobs require an AWS certification rather than a state license. The entry-level credential is AWS D1.1 (structural steel), and code certifications like API 1104 plus advanced process tests (SMAW/TIG, 6G pipe) pay more. Certifications are renewed periodically, which is a big reason trained welders stay in demand.

How much does it cost to become a welder?

Community college welding certificates run about $2,000-$6,000, trade school programs $5,000-$15,000, and union apprenticeships $0 while paying you a wage from day one. Compared to the $100K+ average cost of a four-year degree, welding training is a small, fast investment most graduates pay back within their first year.

How much does a beginner welder make?

Entry-level welders earn about $16-$20 an hour (roughly $33K-$40K a year) right after certification, climbing to the $45K-$70K range for experienced welders. Certified pipe welders (6G) and specialized welders routinely earn $80K-$110K+.

Is becoming a welder worth it in 2026?

Yes for the right person. You can be hirable in about 6-7 months, earn $45K-$70K baseline, and push past $80K-$100K with pipe, TIG, or underwater specialization. The honest trade-off is the physical work and the fact that top pay comes from stacking certifications beyond the entry level.

Your Next Move

You now know the five steps from zero to a welding paycheck — faster than almost any other route into the skilled trades. Your next move is simple: pick your path (school or apprenticeship), pick a program, and book your first booth hours.
