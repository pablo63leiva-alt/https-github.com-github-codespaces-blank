Best Work Gloves for Construction

Updated September 16, 2026 · 10 min read · Tool Guides

Your hands take every hit on a job site — rebar, concrete, sharp metal, swinging loads. The right gloves rated for impact, cut, and grip are the cheapest safety purchase you'll make this year.

Gloves are the piece of PPE beginners ignore the most — and the one that saves you the most grief. A $20 pair with impact padding will stop a falling length of rebar from smashing the back of your hand. A $12 nitrile-coated pair keeps you gripping a soaked shovel on an 8-ounce framing day. Every land it. We rounded up the construction gloves that actually earn their keep, from the $35 impact-rated daily driver to the $10 budget pair you buy five of.

Here's the honest starting point: most construction sites only mandate basic cut and abrasion resistance, so impact protection is your call. That's not a reason to skip it. Hands are the most-injured body part in construction, and the damage is almost always to knuckles and fingertips — exactly the zones impact-rated back-of-hand padding, reinforced palms, and double-layer fingertips protect. Skilled trade careers in framing, ironworking, masonry, and general construction routinely start at $15-$25/hr, and journeymen in these trades pull $45K-$80K — a $40 glove budget is nothing against that paycheck, and one saved trip to urgent care pays for a year of gloves.

Why the Right Gloves Matter More Than You Think

Unlike boots, gloves wear out on a schedule you can feel. Rebar and block chew through thin knit palms in days. Concrete dust and wet mortar turn leather stiff. Snagged cuffs shred in power tools. So the real question isn't "which glove?" — it's "which glove for which job?" Framing, concrete, structural steel, and welding each punish different parts of the glove, and the best pick for one trade is a bad pick for another.

Three ratings matter most, and we explain each below so you can read labels like a pro: ANSI/ISEA 138 (impact), ANSI/ISEA 105 cut levels A1-A9, and grip/coating type for wet and oily conditions. Once you know those three, you can buy confident from any brand.

How to Read Glove Safety Ratings

ANSI/ISEA 138: Impact

ANSI/ISEA 138 tests how much force transmits through the back of the hand and knuckles during a sharp blow. Gloves pass at performance Level 1, 2, or 3 based on how much of the strike the padding absorbs — Level 3 is the most protective. Pair that with ANSI/ISEA 105 for cut, and you've covered the two ratings that matter most on a construction site.

Cut-Resistance Levels A1-A9 (The Rough Sense)

The ANSI/ISEA 105 scale runs A1 to A9, and the sense of it is simple:

  • A1-A3: Light — snags, scrapes, masonry dust, general handling. This is standard utility-glove territory.
  • A4-A6: Sharp edges — rebar, wire, metal studs, block edges. This is where most construction work lives.
  • A7-A9: Maximum — heavy, sustained contact with sharp stock like glass, coil steel, and sheet metal. Expect stiffer, thicker, hotter gloves.

Tip: don't buy A9 for everything. Higher cut levels mean stiffer fibers and worse dexterity, so match the level to the task — and remember cut resistance protects against cuts, not punctures or crush. That's what impact armor and reinforced fingertips are for.

Grip: Wet, Oily & Coated Palms

Palm coating decides how gloves behave when conditions turn wet, and it's written right on the label once you know the code:

  • Foam nitrile: the best all-rounder — grips on wet and oily surfaces, anti-snag, breathable. Buy this for general construction.
  • Solid nitrile/rubber dip: the wet-work specialist — sheds water and chemical splash, great for concrete and washing tasks, heavier and hotter.
  • PVC: durable and oily-grip, used on waterproof dip gloves, less dexterous.
  • Leather and uncoated knit: dry-work tools — once wet, they soak, chill, and tear. Keep them for framing and tool work, not trench work.

When a site warns about "wet conditions," that's shorthand for: pick the coated-palm glove, not the leather one.

How to Size Work Gloves (Measure, Don't Guess)

Fit is where most people go wrong — loose gloves get caught in drills and cause half the fatigue complaints on a site. Here's the 30-second method:

  • Wrap a soft measuring tape around your dominant hand's palm, just below the knuckles (don't include the thumb).
  • Match that number to the brand's size chart. Roughly, 7-8 in = S/M, 8-9 in = L, 9-10 in = XL, 10-11 in = XXL — but every brand differs, so check the chart on the listing.
  • Fingertips should reach the ends of the glove with no more than a sliver of slack — snug enough to pinch a small object, loose enough to make a fist comfortably.
  • Remember leather and reinforced fabric stretch with break-in. Buy the snug size so the glove molds to your hand instead of bagging out in week two.

When to Replace Your Gloves

Gloves fail silently. The padding that looks fine may already be crushed flat inside a leather shell, and a split seam on a welding glove means the next spark lands on skin. Replace gloves when you see any of these:

  • Holes or worn-through palm coating — the protective layer is gone.
  • Impact padding that's flattened, cracked, or visibly compressed — it's not absorbing the next hit.
  • Broken seams or stitching — the last barrier between you and the sharp edge.
  • Stiff, cracked leather or heat damage on welding gloves.
  • Gloves that stay wet inside — insulation and coating failure.

Heavy-use pairs — concrete, masonry, rebar — realistically last a few weeks to a few months. Check your gloves at the start of every shift and keep a spare pair in the truck. A $10 replacement is cheaper than a $200 urgent care visit.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does ANSI/ISEA 138 impact rating on a glove mean?

ANSI/ISEA 138 tests how well a glove cushions the knuckles and back of the hand against sharp blows, from falling objects to swinging rebar. Gloves pass at performance Level 1, 2, or 3 based on the amount of force transmitted through the padding, with Level 3 absorbing the most. Impact-rated gloves are not required on every site, but they are cheap insurance for anyone doing concrete, demolition, structural steel, or heavy material handling.

What do cut-resistance levels A1 to A9 mean?

ANSI/ISEA 105 cut ratings range from A1 (lowest) to A9 (highest). A1-A3 covers light snag and abrasion resistance, A4-A6 handles sharper-edge materials like rebar, formwork, and sheet metal, and A7-A9 is for heavy, sustained-edge contact such as glass or metal coil handling. Higher levels use stiffer fibers, so they trade some dexterity for protection — match the level to the task instead of buying maximum cut protection for everything.

How should construction work gloves fit?

Work gloves should fit snugly across the palm and fingers with no excess fabric bunching — loose gloves are what get caught in tools and add to hand fatigue. Measure the circumference of your dominant hand around the palm (just below the knuckles) and compare it to the brand's size chart; sizes usually run S, M, L, XL, and XXL. Leather and fabric-lined gloves stretch with break-in, so choose the snug size, not the roomy one.

When should I replace my work gloves?

Replace gloves the moment you see holes or worn-through palm coating, broken seams, cracked leather, crushed impact padding, or stitching that no longer holds. Once the protective layer is compromised, the glove gives you a false sense of safety. As a rule of thumb, heavy-use pairs — concrete, masonry, rebar — last a few weeks to a few months, so keep a spare pair in the truck and inspect your gloves at the start of every shift.

What gloves are best for wet conditions on a construction site?

For wet work, choose a nitrile, PVC, or rubber-coated palm — these coatings grip when wet and shed water instead of soaking it up. Foam-nitrite coatings add grip on oily surfaces, and full nitrile dip gloves (sometimes latex or PVC) keep hands dry for digging, washing, and concrete work. Skip plain leather and uncoated knit in wet conditions — they get heavy, cold, and slippery, and they tear faster once soaked.

Your Next Step

Buy by the job, not by the brand hype: an impact-rated pair for mixed work, an A4-A6 cut pair for sharp materials, a coated-palm pair for wet days, a leather pair for welding, and a cheap multi-pack for the mundane hauling work. Write the ANSI levels you need on a notecard in your glove box — you'll be able to read any label on the shelf in 30 seconds.
