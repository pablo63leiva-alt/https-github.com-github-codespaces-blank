HVAC Apprentice Salary: The Year-by-Year Pay Breakdown

Updated September 11, 2026 · 8 min read · Career Guide

HVAC apprentices earn real wages from week one, with scheduled raises every year of a 3-5 year program. Here's exactly what a first-year HVAC apprentice makes in 2026, how pay climbs year by year, what the EPA 608 means for your career, and the job outlook that keeps this trade in demand.

Search "HVAC apprentice salary" and you'll find a wide spread of numbers — some say $15 an hour, others claim $30, and a few promise six figures within a couple years. All of them can be accurate, because where you train, whether you go union or non-union, and which certifications you hold swing the number by thousands each year. Here's the real, year-by-year version.

The headline first: HVAC apprentices earn while they learn. You start at roughly 40-50% of a journeyman wage and get bumps on a published schedule. Most registered HVAC apprenticeships run 3-5 years, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a median annual pay of $57,310 for fully licensed HVAC technicians — that's roughly $27-$30/hr before overtime or specialization. Top earners push past $70,000+. This guide walks you through every step, one year at a time.

The Short Answer: You Get Paid From Day One

The single biggest difference between HVAC training and a four-year degree: a degree bills you, an apprenticeship pays you. In 2026, first-year HVAC apprentices typically earn between $15 and $20 an hour depending on market and union. By year three, most apprentices sit between $20 and $26 an hour — near 80-90% of journeyman scale. Licensed HVAC technicians land at the BLS median of $57,310 a year, and technicians specializing in commercial refrigeration, building automation, or heat pump installation regularly push past that. Union vs Non-Union: What Actually Changes

Union HVAC apprenticeships (typically through UA locals) come with published scale rates, guaranteed raises, pension and health benefits, and training funded through the apprenticeship program — zero tuition out of your pocket. Non-union shops vary more: some match or approach union rates and benefits, others offer just the paycheck. The key difference in practice isn't always the hourly rate — it's the benefits package. A union apprentice at $18/hr with full health coverage, a pension contribution, and funded classroom hours is frequently ahead of a non-union apprentice at $20/hr with no benefits behind it. One number that doesn't get enough attention: total compensation. When you factor in health insurance ($5,000-$10,000/year value), pension contributions, and paid training, the union advantage can be worth $5,000-$15,000 a year beyond the hourly rate. Over a 4-year apprenticeship, that's a significant difference in real financial security.

The EPA 608: Your Ticket to the Trade

Every HVAC technician needs the EPA 608 certification — it's a federal requirement for purchasing, handling, or disposing of refrigerants used in air conditioning and refrigeration systems. The good news: it's one of the fastest certifications to earn. You study the material (often 2-4 weeks of self-paced prep), pass an EPA-approved exam, and you're certified. The four types are:

  • Type I: Small appliances (refrigerators, window units)
  • Type II: High-pressure systems (most residential AC and heat pumps)
  • Type III: Low-pressure systems (large commercial chillers)
  • Universal: All three types — the one most employers want

The Universal certification is the smart move. Most HVAC employers require it, and it takes the same exam session as any single type. The Resources page lists the certifications that matter by trade, including where to find EPA-approved testing centers.

What Boosts an HVAC Apprentice's Pay the Fastest

Your step raises are guaranteed, but these levers move the needle much faster:

  • Overtime. HVAC work routinely pays time-and-a-half, especially during peak summer and winter seasons. Emergency service calls often hit double-time. A few extra hours a week during busy months quietly adds thousands to the table above.
  • Getting your EPA 608 Universal. Many employers pay a premium — $0.50-$2.00/hr more — for technicians who hold the Universal certification versus a single type.
  • Specialties. Commercial refrigeration, building automation controls, VRF systems, and heat pump installation all pay premiums over standard residential service work.
  • NATE certification. The North American Technician Excellence certification is the industry's most recognized credential. NATE-certified technicians often earn 5-15% more than non-certified peers.
  • Master license (later). Several states offer master HVAC licenses that let you pull permits, run your own business, and hire apprentices — that's when the hourly ceiling stops applying.

None of these require a four-year degree. The step-by-step roadmap for each one is in our Getting Started guide.

Job Outlook: Why HVAC Is One of the Most Stable Trades

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 5% growth for HVAC technicians through 2033, with about 36,700 openings each year from new positions and replacement needs. That's faster than average for all occupations, and the demand drivers are structural — not cyclical:

  • Every new building needs heating and cooling systems installed and maintained
  • Older systems require regular service and replacement
  • Energy-efficiency regulations drive upgrades and retrofits
  • Heat pump adoption is accelerating under federal incentives
  • Data centers, grow facilities, and commercial refrigeration all need specialized HVAC work

Unlike some trades that depend heavily on new construction cycles, HVAC service work happens year-round regardless of the economy. Every occupied building needs climate control.

The Apprenticeship Path, Step by Step

HVAC apprenticeship sounds complicated until you see it as five straightforward steps:

1

Meet the Base Requirements

Most registered HVAC apprenticeships want you to be at least 18, hold a high school diploma or GED, and pass basic math. A driver's license helps — you'll be driving to job sites. No prior HVAC experience is required.

2

Apply to a Registered Apprenticeship or Trade School Program

Local UA (United Association) locals run the big union programs; apprenticeship.gov lists registered programs in every state. Community college HVAC programs (6-24 months) are another common entry point, often running alongside or before an apprenticeship.

3

Earn Your EPA 608 Certification

This is the one certification you need before (or shortly after) starting work. Study for 2-4 weeks, pass the exam, and you're ready to handle refrigerants on the job. Most employers require it within your first 90 days.

4

Log Hours and Pass Your Classes

Stack roughly 6,000-8,000 on-the-job hours plus classroom instruction (often 144+ hours a year). Each year you progress a step, and each step raises your hourly rate per the schedule above.

5

Get Licensed, Then Level Up

Finish the hours, pass your state's licensing exam, and you're a licensed HVAC technician earning the BLS median of $57,310 or more. From there: specialize in commercial systems, earn your NATE certification, or move toward a master license and your own business.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does an HVAC apprentice make in 2026?

Typically $15-$30 an hour depending on year, region, and union vs non-union. First-year HVAC apprentices start around $15-$20/hr, second-year apprentices reach $17-$23/hr, and licensed HVAC technicians land at a median of $57,310 a year — roughly $27-$30/hr — before overtime or specialization premiums.

How long is an HVAC apprenticeship?

Most registered HVAC apprenticeships run 3-5 years, combining on-the-job training with classroom instruction. Many technicians also complete a 6-24 month trade school or community college program before or during their apprenticeship. The EPA 608 certification can be earned in weeks and is required for handling refrigerants.

Do HVAC apprentices get paid during training?

Yes. Apprentices earn a wage from day one, starting at roughly 40-50% of journeyman scale with scheduled raises every 6-12 months. Union apprenticeship programs cost $0 in tuition while paying you. By the time you reach journeyman status, you are earning a full professional wage with zero student debt.

What is the EPA 608 certification?

The EPA 608 certification is a federal requirement for anyone who purchases, handles, or disposes of refrigerants used in HVAC systems. It comes in four types (Type I, II, III, and Universal) and can be earned by passing an EPA-approved exam. Most HVAC employers require it, and the Universal certification covers all equipment types.

What is the job outlook for HVAC technicians?

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 5% growth for HVAC technicians through 2033, with about 36,700 openings each year from growth and replacement needs. The constant demand for heating, cooling, and refrigeration in homes, businesses, and data centers makes HVAC one of the most stable trade careers available.
