How to Become a Plumber: The Step-by-Step Path for 2026

Updated September 14, 2026 · 8 min read · Career Guide

Plumbers earn a median of $56,000-$74,000 a year with zero student loans. Here's the complete step-by-step path from high school to licensed plumber — apprenticeship timeline, real certifications, year-by-year earnings, and how to start with no experience.

If you've ever searched "how to become a plumber," you've probably waded through a swamp of vague advice and outdated salary numbers. The real answer is simpler than most sites make it: you need a registered apprenticeship, a few thousand hours of paid training, some classroom instruction, and a license. No four-year degree. No six-figure debt. And you get paid starting from day one.

This guide breaks down every step in order. You'll get the real 2026 salary numbers, the certifications that actually matter, how long each phase takes, and exactly what to do if you have zero plumbing experience. Everything is based on Bureau of Labor Statistics data and current registered apprenticeship requirements.

Do Plumbers Actually Make Good Money?

Yes — and the numbers keep climbing. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a median plumber salary of $55K-$75K a year depending on experience, location, and whether you're union or non-union. Master plumbers and business owners regularly clear $80K-$100K+, and emergency, industrial, and gas-line specialists can push past $100K.

The key detail nobody mentions upfront: you earn money as a first-year apprentice. While a college student is paying $30K-$60K a year for classes, a plumber apprentice starts at roughly $16-$22 per hour and gets scheduled raises every 6-12 months. By year four, you're earning $24-$30 per hour — already above the median household income in most states — and you haven't spent a dime on tuition.

"While your classmates are taking on $40K+ in debt, you'll be earning a real wage toward a license that pays $60K-$74K from year one — and the ceiling only goes up."

Plumbing isn't glamorous, but it's one of the highest-paying trade careers you can start with a high school diploma. The full breakdown of how plumber pay stacks up against other trades is on our Trades page, which covers 12 careers with salary ranges, growth projections, and training timelines.

What Does a Plumber Actually Do?

A plumber installs, maintains, and repairs the piping systems that carry water, gas, and waste through buildings. It's part mechanical work, part problem-solving, and a lot of reading blueprints and local codes. Most plumbers specialize in one of these lanes:

  • Residential: installing fixtures in homes, running water lines, repairing leaks, and servicing water heaters.
  • Commercial: office buildings, restaurants, hospitals — larger piping systems, medical gas lines, and code-heavy installations.
  • Industrial: factories, power plants, refineries — high-pressure piping, steam systems, and process piping. Often the highest pay.
  • Service and Repair: emergency calls, drain cleaning, and fixture replacement. High demand, flexible hours.
  • Gasfitting: installing and repairing gas lines for heating, cooking, and industrial equipment. Requires additional certification in most states.

Every lane pays, but gasfitting and industrial plumbing consistently top the salary charts. Want to compare plumber pay against other high-paying trades? Our Trades page breaks down all 12 careers side by side.

The 7-Step Path to Becoming a Plumber

1

Build the Right Foundation in High School

You don't need perfect grades, but a few classes make the path dramatically easier. Math (algebra, geometry, measurement) matters because plumbing work involves calculating pipe sizes, pressure, and flow rates. Physics helps you understand water pressure and fluid dynamics. If your school offers shop classes or vocational tech programs, take them — they give you hands-on skills and show employers you're serious.

Get your driver's license early. You'll drive to job sites daily, and most plumbing contractors treat it as a requirement. Any part-time work with a plumbing contractor — even as a helper — gives you a massive head start when you apply for an apprenticeship. See our complete Getting Started guide for the full roadmap on prep, tools, and landing your first trade job.

2

Choose Your Entry Route: Apprenticeship or Trade School

This is the fork in the road, and it's worth understanding before you spend anything. Union apprenticeships — typically through UA (United Association) locals — are free or low-cost. You work paid hours and attend classroom instruction funded by the program. Trade school programs at community colleges cost roughly $5,000-$15,000 and provide structured classroom training in plumbing theory, code, and blueprint reading.

Both work. The apprenticeship is the cheapest and most proven route — you earn while you learn. Trade school can be a smart head start if your local apprenticeship has a long waitlist or if you want to come in with stronger classroom knowledge. Many plumbers do both: trade school first, then apprenticeship. We break down the money on each path in our guide to trade school vs college.

3

Apply for a Registered Apprenticeship

Search apprenticeship.gov for registered plumbing programs in your state, and contact your local UA hall. The typical application asks for a high school diploma or GED, proof you're at least 18 (some programs accept 16-17 with parental consent), and a basic math aptitude test.

Once accepted, you work under licensed journeymen, log your training hours, and earn scheduled raises. A typical plumbing apprenticeship runs 4-5 years and requires roughly 8,000 on-the-job hours plus 144+ hours of classroom instruction per year. You get paid the entire time.

4

Stack Essential Certifications

While you're in the apprenticeship, earn credentials that make you more valuable to every employer. The non-negotiables are:

  • OSHA 10 or OSHA 30: safety credentials every construction employer recognizes.
  • Backflow Prevention Certification: required in most jurisdictions for testing and installing backflow preventers.
  • NCCER Plumbing Credentials: nationally recognized, portable training certifications.
  • Medical Gas Installer (later): specialized certification for hospital and healthcare facility piping.

Each certification adds to your hourly value and opens new job opportunities. The Resources page lists which certifications matter most and where to study for them.

5

Get Licensed as a Journeyman Plumber

After completing your apprenticeship hours, you sit for your state's journeyman plumber licensing exam. This is the credential that moves you into full professional pay. The exam typically covers plumbing code, pipe sizing, fixture installation, drainage systems, and local code requirements. Licensing rules vary by state, so check your local licensing board for the exact hour requirements and exam content.

Once you pass, you're earning a full journeyman wage — the BLS median is $55K-$75K, and many licensed plumbers push well past that with overtime and specialization.

6

Level Up: Master License and Specialization

A journeyman license is a starting line, not a finish line. After 2-5 years as a journeyman, you can test for a master plumber license, which lets you pull permits, run your own jobs, hire apprentices, and start your own business. Specializations like gasfitting, medical gas, industrial process piping, and green plumbing (rainwater harvesting, solar thermal) all command premium rates.

Master plumbers who run their own shops commonly earn $80K-$150K+ depending on the size of their operation and their market. The ceiling is what you make of it.

7

Never Stop Building

Plumbing codes update every few years. New technologies — tankless water heaters, PEX piping, smart leak detectors, water-recycling systems — keep the trade evolving. The plumbers who earn the most are the ones who keep learning: new certifications, new specialties, and a willingness to tackle the jobs other plumbers turn down.

Every hour you work and every certification you earn raises your ceiling. That's the difference between a job and a career.

What Makes Plumber Pay Go Up Faster

Your step raises are built in, but these levers move the needle much faster:

  • Overtime. Plumbing emergencies don't wait. Emergency service calls pay time-and-a-half or double-time. A few extra hours a week during peak seasons quietly adds thousands to your annual income.
  • Gasfitting certification. In states where gasfitting is a separate license, this certification opens a higher-paying specialization. Gasfitters often earn $5-$10/hr more than general plumbers.
  • Medical gas certification. Hospitals and healthcare facilities pay premium rates for plumbers certified to install and maintain medical gas piping systems.
  • Commercial and industrial work. Residential plumbing pays well; commercial and industrial piping pays better. Bigger systems, stricter codes, higher skill requirements — and higher hourly rates.
  • Starting your own business. A master plumber license lets you pull permits, hire apprentices, and set your own rates. The income ceiling disappears.

The "No Student Debt" Math

A four-year college graduate walks out with roughly $37,000+ in student loans — and that's before interest. A plumber apprentice in the same four-to-five-year window has $0 in student debt and four-to-five years of paid, licensed-track experience. That's a $40K-$80K difference in net financial position by age 24, depending on the college route.

How to Choose the Right Plumber Apprenticeship

Not all apprenticeships are equal. Here's what to look for when you're comparing programs:

  • Registered with the DOL. A registered apprenticeship means your hours count toward a nationally recognized credential. Check apprenticeship.gov.
  • Union vs. non-union. Union programs typically offer published scale rates, guaranteed benefits, and funded classroom hours. Non-union programs vary more, but some match union quality. Ask about benefits, health insurance, and pension contributions — not just the hourly rate.
  • Hour count and timeline. Most states require 8,000+ hours. Ask how long the program takes and what the raise schedule looks like.
  • Classroom hours. Quality programs include 144+ hours of classroom instruction per year. Ask who teaches — journeyman instructors or college adjuncts matter.
  • Job placement rate. A good program places nearly all graduates as journeymen. Ask the program's completion rate.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to become a licensed plumber?

Most states require 4-5 years of apprenticeship training, including roughly 8,000 on-the-job hours plus classroom instruction. After completing the apprenticeship, you pass a state licensing exam to become a journeyman plumber.

How much do plumbers make in 2026?

The median plumber salary is $56,000-$74,000 a year depending on experience, specialization, and location. Master plumbers and business owners commonly earn $80K-$100K+, and emergency or industrial plumbers can push past $100K.

Do plumber apprentices get paid?

Yes, from day one. First-year plumber apprentices typically earn $16-$22 per hour, with scheduled raises every 6-12 months. By year four, most apprentices reach $24-$30 per hour before licensing.

Do I need to go to trade school to become a plumber?

Not necessarily. Many plumbers enter through registered apprenticeships with zero tuition. Trade school or community college programs can give you a head start with classroom instruction, but the apprenticeship itself is the primary training path and it pays you while you learn.

What certifications do plumbers need?

The essential credential is a journeyman plumber license, issued by your state after completing apprenticeship hours and passing an exam. OSHA 10 or OSHA 30 safety certification is also widely required. Many plumbers also pursue backflow prevention certification, medical gas certification, or a master plumber license to open their own business.
