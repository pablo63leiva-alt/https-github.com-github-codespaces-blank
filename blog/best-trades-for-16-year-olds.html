Best Trades for 16-Year-Olds: Start Before Your Friends Even Pick a Major

Updated September 10, 2026 · 6 min read · Career Guide

You don't have to wait until 18 to start a career in the trades. There are 750,000 open trade jobs in the U.S. right now, and a lot of them will take a motivated 16-year-old — paid, trained, and ahead of the debt curve.

Here's what most career advice gets wrong: it treats 16 as "too early to start" and hands you a four-year waiting list instead. The trades treat 16 as a hiring advantage. Between work permits, pre-apprenticeship programs, and youth apprenticeships that start as young as 16-17 depending on your state, you can be earning, training, and stacking certifications before your classmates pick a study abroad program.

This guide covers the best trades for 16-year-olds, what you can actually do today (not "someday"), the pay you can expect as you climb, and the five steps to land your first trade job before you turn 18.

Can a 16-Year-Old Really Start a Trade? Yes — Here's What You Can Do Today

A lot of young people assume trades are an adult-only club. False. Here's the reality of what's available to a 16-year-old in most states:

  • Part-time helper jobs: local electrical, plumbing, HVAC, and carpentry contractors routinely hire high schoolers to run material, prep sites, and learn on the job. Pay usually runs $12-$18 an hour.
  • Pre-apprenticeships: short, structured programs (often free, sometimes paid) that teach safety basics and tool skills. Several accept teens 16+ with a parent's sign-off.
  • Youth apprenticeships: registered programs designed for teens, combining high school classes with paid work hours. Starting ages vary by state — commonly 16 or 17.
  • Trade school and career center programs: many districts let juniors take welding, electrical, auto, and construction classes for free during the school day.
  • OSHA 10 certification: no federal minimum age to take OSHA 10 — though some providers set their own (often 16-18), and it's the credential every contractor recognizes. You can knock it out online.

The pattern is simple: you don't need to be an adult to start building skills — you just need to start. That's the whole head start in one sentence.

The Best Trades for 16-Year-Olds (Ranked by How Fast You Can Start)

Not every trade lets a teen in the door at the same speed. These are the ones with the best mix of "they'll hire a 16-year-old," "you can train cheap or free," and "the pay gets serious fast."

  • Welding: the fastest on the list to a real credential. Many welding programs run 6-7 months, and AWS (American Welding Society) certifications are respected as soon as you pass the test. We wrote the full story in our guide to whether welding is a good career — short version: $45K-$70K baseline, more with specialization.
  • Electrician: probably the highest long-term ceiling. You can start as a paid helper at 16, then roll into a registered apprenticeship at 18. Median pay is around $61,600, and the four-step path is laid out in how to become an electrician.
  • Plumbing & HVAC: local shops are chronically short-handed and love teen helpers who show up on time. Both pay $50K+ early in a career and can't be outsourced.
  • Carpentry & construction: the most entry-friendly. Framers, trim carpenters, and general contractors hire young labor on almost every site, and you'll learn a little of every trade by osmosis.
  • Automotive & diesel mechanics: shops will absolutely hire a 16-year-old for oil changes, tires, and shop cleanup — and most will train you into the bays within a year or two. Full mechanics clear $45K-$65K.
  • Landscaping & irrigation: the easiest seasonal entry, tons of small-business owners hiring, and it feeds directly into plumbing-adjacent irrigation work.

Why Starting at 16 Is a Head Start, Not a Fallback

The average four-year college graduate walks out the door owing about $37,000 in student loans — and that's the average, meaning plenty owe far more. Meanwhile, there are roughly 750,000 open skilled-trade positions in the U.S., many of them paying $60,000+ without a degree.

Now add the age advantage. Everyone you know is on the same four-year timer. If you start trade training at 16 instead of 22, you're not just saving money — you're getting paid during the exact years your peers are paying tuition.

"Start at 16 and you'll have a license or certificate by 20. Start at 22 and you'll have student debt. Same effort, opposite balance sheet."

The full math — costs, debt, time-to-first-paycheck, and five-year earnings — is in our comparison of trade school vs college. The gap is bigger than most people believe.

What You Can Do at Every Age: 14 to 18

Age What's Realistically Open to You
14-15 Work permits for light jobs, school shop classes, volunteer or shadow days with local contractors, summer yard-maintenance work.
16 Part-time helper and laborer jobs, many pre-apprenticeships, youth apprenticeships in states that allow 16, OSHA 10 certification, community-college dual enrollment.
17 Everything above, plus more states open formal apprenticeships at 17 with parental consent, and trade school programs widen their teen admissions.
18 Full registered apprenticeships, most union entry points, driver's license requirements met, and the ability to run job-site equipment.

Age rules vary by state and by employer. The exact start age for registered youth apprenticeships differs across the U.S., so check your state's apprenticeship office — but the strategic play is the same everywhere: start earning early either way.

5 Realistic Steps to Land Your First Trade Job Before 18

1

Load Up on the Right High School Classes

Trade math is a lot easier when algebra and geometry don't scare you. Physics helps you understand everything from electrical circuits to welding heat. Shop, drafting, and CTE classes are the obvious wins — take them all if your school offers them.

Also get your driver's license as soon as you can. The ability to drive to a job site is a genuine screening requirement for a lot of teen-friendly trade jobs.

2

Get Your OSHA 10 Card

It's the single cheapest, fastest credential that makes you look serious to a contractor. You can complete the OSHA 10-Hour General Industry or Construction course online in about a day, and there's no federal minimum age to take it — though some providers set their own (often 16-18). Nobody expects a 16-year-old to have it — which is exactly why it gets you noticed.

3

Find a Part-Time Helper or Pre-Apprenticeship

Walk into local shops and ask, check state youth-apprenticeship programs, and search apprenticeship.gov for pre-apprenticeships near you. Even two afternoons a week adds up fast: by 18 you'll have real hours, real references, and a paycheck history.

Pro Tip: The full roadmap for landing that first position — from résumé to interview — is on our Getting Started guide.
4

Use Dual Enrollment for Cheap Training

Lots of community colleges and career centers let high schoolers take welding, electrical, or auto classes while still in school — often free or heavily discounted. That's a trade school semester you get for the cost of a bus pass, and the certs you earn are the same ones adults pay thousands for.

5

Convert Your Head Start Into a Full Apprenticeship

The day you turn 18 (or 17, in states that allow it), you apply to formal registered apprenticeships with hours and certifications already in hand. Contractors fight over applicants who show up already trained, already certified, and already dependable. You'll likely start at a higher pay step than a total newcomer.

The 16-to-25 Pay Trajectory (Real Money)

Here's what the head start actually pays, as realistic numbers for a motivated teen who starts at 16 in a helper or youth-apprenticeship role:

Age Stage Typical Earnings
16 Part-time helper / pre-apprentice $12-$18/hr, after-school hours
18 First-year apprentice $17-$24/hr full time, paid training
21 Journey-level or nearly licensed $55K-$75K with overtime
25 Licensed, with specialties or leads $70K-$90K+ and climbing

Ranges are realistic estimates, not guarantees — pay varies by trade, region, and union. But compare any row to $0 earned and $37K+ owed during the same years, and the head start advantage is obvious.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I start an apprenticeship at 16?

In many states, yes. Youth apprenticeships and pre-apprenticeship programs commonly accept 16-17 year olds with parental consent and school release. Full journeyman-style apprenticeships usually require being 18, so teens use that gap to stack paid helper hours, shop classes, and an OSHA 10 card.

What trade jobs can a 16-year-old get without experience?

Plenty: helper and laborer roles, construction cleanup, landscaping and irrigation crews, auto-shop assistants, parts runners, and apprentice-level positions in welding, carpentry, HVAC, and electrical where the employer trains you on the job. Most of these pay $12-$18 an hour part-time during high school.

Do trade jobs pay more than typical teen jobs?

Yes. Fast food and retail often hover near minimum wage. A teen helper or pre-apprentice earns $12-$18 an hour and jumps to $25-$40 an hour in their early 20s once licensed or certified — with zero student loans.

What if my high school doesn't offer shop classes?

You don't need them, though they help. You can take CTE or dual-enrollment trade classes at a nearby community college, sign up for after-school pre-apprenticeships, get your OSHA 10 online, or land a summer helper job with a local contractor. Any of those gets you started before 18.

Your Next Move

You have a real advantage most adults never had: a legitimate on-ramp at 16. The best trades for 16-year-olds are the ones you can actually enter now — welding and construction for speed, electrical and plumbing for ceiling, and help or pre-apprenticeship roles to get paid while you figure out your favorite.

Start with one step: take a shop class, grab your OSHA 10, or ask one local contractor if they need help. And if you're still weighing trades against the traditional path, the numbers in our getting-started roadmap and our trade school vs college breakdown will settle it fast.
