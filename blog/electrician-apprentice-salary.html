Electrician Apprentice Salary: The Year-by-Year Pay Breakdown

Updated September 10, 2026 · 8 min read · Career Guide

Forget the "you get paid after four years" college math. Apprentice electricians earn real wages from week one, with scheduled raises every year of a 4-5 year program. Here's exactly what a first-year apprentice makes in 2026, how pay climbs year by year, what boosts your rate faster, and the honest first-year reality.

Search "electrician apprentice salary" and you'll get a wall of conflicting numbers: one person says $15 an hour, another says $30, a third claims they cleared six figures by year two. All of them can be telling the truth — because where you train (union hall or non-union shop) and which state you're in swing the number by thousands of dollars a year. Here's the real, year-by-year version.

The headline first: apprentices earn while they learn. You start at roughly 40-50% of a journey-level wage and get bumps on a published schedule. Most registered electrician apprenticeships run 4-5 years, log around 8,000 on-the-job hours, and finish at journey-level pay of $60K-$80K+ a year — before overtime. That's the payoff this guide walks you to, one year at a time.

The Short Answer: You Get Paid From Day One

The single biggest difference between electrician training and a four-year degree: a degree bills you, an apprenticeship pays you. In 2026, first-year apprentice electricians typically earn between $15 and $22 an hour depending on market and union. By year four, most apprentices sit between $20 and $32 an hour — near 80-90% of journeyman scale. Experienced, licensed journey-level electricians land in the $60K-$80K range nationally, and electricians specializing in industrial, lineman, solar, or EV work regularly push past that. The full electrician picture — including why it's the top licensed trade on our board — is in our guide to how to become an electrician.

Here's the table everyone really wants. Rows are apprentice years; columns separate local union (IBEW) scale from typical non-union pay, with full-time annual equivalents for both.

*Annual = hourly × ~2,000 hours, before overtime. Apprentice schedules also include classroom hours, so take-home varies. Ranges are realistic 2026 estimates across union contracts and non-union markets — not guarantees. Compare electrician pay against the other 11 trades on our Trades page.

How Your State Changes the Numbers

Electrician wages are set locally, and the spread between the hottest local and a small-town shop is real. Union scale comes from each IBEW local's contract; non-union pay tracks the local job market. Directionally, think in three bands:

State Band Example Locals Typical Year-1 Hourly Journey-Level Hourly
High-paying locals CA, NY, MA, WA $18-$22/hr $35-$40+/hr
Middle of the road TX, FL, MN, OH, CO $16-$19/hr $28-$34/hr
Lower cost-of-living MS, AL, AR, WV $15-$17/hr $25-$30/hr

Example states and ranges are directional 2026 estimates, not official quotes — every local union contract and every state licensing board is different. Skip the spreadsheet and call the nearest IBEW hall directly.

The Total Package Hiding Inside Those Hourly Numbers

Every pay table hides something: the benefits. On a union (IBEW) job, part of the contract's total compensation usually lands in a health plan, a pension or annuity, and a training fund that covers your classroom hours — money that never shows up on your check but quietly adds up to thousands each year in avoided healthcare bills and retirement savings. Non-union shops vary more: some carry the same package, others little beyond the paycheck. So when you compare "apprentice wages by year," ask what the whole package is worth, not just the rate. A $19 union rate is frequently worth more than a $22 non-union rate with no benefits behind it. It also changes the year-by-year math in your favor. A year-1 apprentice at $17-$22/hr grosses roughly $34K-$44K on a standard 40-hour week — real money, and more importantly money you didn't borrow. Run a rough four-year comparison: a four-year degree frequently costs over $100K in tuition, fees, and forgone earnings, financed at today's student-loan rates. A 4-5 year apprenticeship pays you from week one, and adding up the annual columns above gives roughly $165K-$215K in cumulative take-home pay while you earn toward the license — then drops you at year four sitting near 80-90% of journeyman scale instead of entering the entry-level job market. Same four years, opposite sides of the ledger.

Those cumulative figures are rough annual illustrations at full-time hours, not quotes — overtime, time off, and your specific market all move the totals. But the direction is identical everywhere: this is the one path that pays you to train, which is exactly why so many electricians hit $60K+ by their mid-20s with zero student debt. What Boosts an Apprentice's Pay the Fastest

Your step raises are guaranteed, but these five levers move the needle much faster:

  • Overtime. Construction work routinely pays time-and-a-half, and weekend, night, and call-out shifts hit double-time. A few overtime Fridays a month quietly adds thousands to the table above.
  • Getting your license. Passing the journeyman exam is the single biggest raise of your career — it takes you from the year-4 scale to full journey-level pay (the $60K-$80K+ row) in one step.
  • Specialties. Industrial controls, lineman work, solar and battery storage, EV charging, and commercial service pay premiums over plain residential work. Trade school vs college shows why this specialty premium compounds so fast.
  • Travel and out-of-town work. Union apprentices can chase per-diem and travel-bonus jobs that pay above local scale.
  • The master license (later). A master electrician license lets you pull permits and run your own jobs — that's when the hourly ceiling stops applying.

None of these require a four-year degree. The step-by-step roadmap for each one is in our Getting Started guide.

One more way to read the table: overtime compounds faster than step raises. Take a year-1 apprentice at roughly $18/hr who averages 3-4 extra hours a week at time-and-a-half — that's about $4,000-$5,500 a year, a raise you didn't have to negotiate. By year 4, the same standing habit moves a $20-$32/hr rate toward journeyman earnings far sooner than the published step schedule alone. Overtime is optional on some sites and expected on others, so ask how your local shops treat it before you sign on.

The 5-Step Apprenticeship Path, Recapped

Apprenticeship sounds complicated until you see it as five straightforward steps:

1

Meet the Base Requirements

Most registered apprenticeships want you to be at least 18 (16-17 is possible in some programs with parental consent), hold a high school diploma or GED, and pass basic algebra. A driver's license helps — you'll be traveling to job sites.

2

Apply to a Registered Apprenticeship

Local IBEW halls and the Electrical Training Alliance run the big union programs; apprenticeship.gov lists registered programs in every state. Expect an aptitude test and an interview, then a ranking on the list.

3

Start Working as a First-Year Apprentice

Day one is paid. You work under licensed journeymen, learn the tools and the code, and log hours toward your license. The pay table above starts right here.

4

Log Hours and Pass Your Classes

Stack roughly 8,000 on-the-job hours plus classroom instruction (often 144+ hours a year). Each year you progress a step, and each step raises your hourly rate per the schedule above.

5

Test for Your Journeyman License, Then Level Up

Finish the hours, pass the state exam, and you're a licensed journeyman at $60K-$80K+. From there: specialize, move toward project management, or earn a master license and run your own business.

Honest: What Your First Year Is Actually Like

Reality check, because the pay tables only tell half the story. As a first-year apprentice you're the lowest person on the site — and you should be. Expect to carry material, dig trenches, pull wire through tight chases, organize the truck, and handle scut work the journeymen don't want. You'll get dirty, your hands will hurt, and mornings come early.

But here's what you won't do: pay tuition, load up student loans, or work unpaid. Every one of those tough days pays your adjusted starting scale, counts toward your 8,000 hours, and moves you one step closer to the raise that's already written into the program. The first year is the steepest part of the learning curve and the cheapest you'll ever be as a licensed electrician. Sitting through it on a set schedule beats four years of debt and a job hunt at 22. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does an electrician apprentice make in 2026?

Typically $15-$32 an hour depending on year, region, and union vs non-union. Year-1 apprentices commonly start around $15-$22/hr, year-4 apprentices reach $20-$32/hr ($25-$32/hr on union scale), and licensed journey-level electricians land at $60K-$80K+ a year before overtime.

Do electrician apprentices get paid during training?

Yes, from day one. Apprentices start around 40-50% of journey-level scale and get scheduled raises every year, often every 6-12 months. You earn wages while you learn, and union apprenticeship programs are usually $0 while paying you.

How much do apprentice wages go up each year?

Expect a bump of roughly $2-$6 an hour per year on union scales, tied to your step and documented hours. By year 4, most apprentices sit near 80-90% of journeyman pay before the full jump to licensed scale.

How long does it take an apprentice to become a journeyman electrician?

Typically 4-5 years: about 8,000 on-the-job hours plus classroom instruction, then a state licensing exam. You get paid the whole way through, unlike a degree that charges you for four years.
