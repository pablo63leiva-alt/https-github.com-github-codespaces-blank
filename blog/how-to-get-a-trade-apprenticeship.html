How to Get a Trade Apprenticeship

Updated September 15, 2026 · 10 min read · Career Guides

There are 750,000+ open trade positions and a shortage of workers to fill them — and apprenticeship is the zero-debt door in. Here's the exact 5-step system: who to apply to, what the test looks like, how to nail the interview, and what you'll earn while you learn.

Everyone tells you "get into the trades," but nobody tells you exactly how to get your foot in the door — which application, which test, which hall, which words to say in the interview. Here's the thing most people miss: it's not a lottery. Apprenticeships are a process with known steps, known tests, and known answers. Do the steps in the right order and the odds flip hard in your favor.

First, the Two Routes: Union vs Non-Union

Pick the route before you pick the application, because they have different doors:

  • Union (e.g. IBEW for electricians, UA for plumbers/pipefitters, UWUA for utilities). Apply through the local hall's Joint Apprenticeship Training Committee (JATC). Published pay scale, structured raises, funded classroom time, and a full benefits package. Entrance is gated by an aptitude test and an interview.
  • Non-union / merit shop (ABC, IEC, and independent contractors). Often simpler to enter — many contractors hire helpers or pre-apprentices directly and register their training so hours count. Pay varies more, benefits fewer, but the waitlist is shorter and promotions can be faster.

Registered apprenticeships — union or not — are backed by the U.S. Department of Labor, which means your hours count toward a nationally recognized credential regardless of shop. Search them free at apprenticeship.gov. See how the trade-school route compares in our Trade Schools & Training hub.

The 5-Step System That Gets You In

Step 1: Pick Your Trade and Target 2-3 Programs (Week 1)

Don't apply to one program — apply to two or three in the same season. Programs admit in cohorts, and a missed application window can cost you months. Start with our Find Your Trade quiz to decide electrician vs plumber vs HVAC vs welder, then search apprenticeship.gov and your local halls in parallel.

Before you apply, build the application kit:

  • A clean one-page resume — no job experience required; leadership, sports, volunteering, and "worked summers with my uncle's crew" all count as reliability signals.
  • A driver's license / reliable transport. Most programs ask, and showing up to a 6 AM call matters.
  • OSHA 10 certification (a cheap weekend course) — the single highest-leverage thing a no-experience applicant can add; every employer recognizes it.
  • Basic algebra refresher. The aptitude test is a math test disguised as an entrance exam.

Step 2: Pass the Aptitude Test (Weeks 2-4)

Union halls (IBEW and UA especially) use a standardized aptitude test. It's not designed to fail you — it's designed to prove you can handle the math and reading the job requires. The three sections:

  • Algebra — fractions, percentages, basic equations, and word problems.
  • Reading comprehension — read a technical passage, answer what it actually says.
  • Mechanical reasoning (varies by program) — gears, levers, pulleys, and simple force questions.

Spend 2-3 weeks practicing timed algebra. Free practice tests are available from the halls themselves and from third-party prep sites. A passing score on the test is the single biggest filter — most applicants who fail do so here, not at the interview.

Step 3: Nail the Interview (Week 4-6)

After the test, the JATC panel interviews you. They are not looking for a trades expert — they are looking for a safe, reliable trainee. The questions they actually ask:

Question What They're Really Asking A Strong Answer
"Why do you want this trade?" Will you stay, or leave at year one? Specific, honest, tied to the work ("I like troubleshooting and the pay path") — not "I heard it pays well."
"Tell us about a time you showed up for something." Reliability. Attendance is 80% of apprenticeship success. A concrete story: sports team you never missed, a job you held through high school, a project you finished.
"Are you okay with working outside, early starts, and physical work?" Do you actually understand the job? "I know it's 6 AM starts and weather doesn't cancel. That's part of why I'm applying."
"What are your long-term plans?" Ambition within the trade (foreman, own truck) Journeyman → maybe running crews or your own shop. Not "this is temporary until I find better."

Step 4: Secure a Seat and Start Earning (Months 1-3)

Accepted applicants get ranked on a list; when a contractor calls, you go to orientation and begin. Key facts so nothing surprises you:

  • Apprentices earn from day one — $15-$25/hr in year one across most trades, climbing to $21-$38/hr by year four. (Union scale often starts a step higher.)
  • Programs run 3-5 years depending on trade (electricians typically 4-5, plumbing 4-5, HVAC 3-4).
  • You accrue 2,000 on-the-job hours a year plus ~144 hours of classroom instruction.
  • Raises are scheduled on a published scale every 6-12 months — no need to negotiate.

Want the year-by-year numbers? See our apprentice wages by year breakdown for six trades, and the trade-specific salary guides for electricians, plumbers, and HVAC techs.

Step 5: Stack the First-Year Advantages (Months 3-12)

  • Show up early, every day. Attendance is the #1 differentiator in year one — crews forgive slow learners, never no-shows.
  • Buy tools on a schedule. See the Trade Tools Guide for the per-trade starter-kit breakdown. Buy the day-one list, phase the rest with your raises.
  • Ask for the classroom work. Volunteers for the education get the better rotations faster.
  • Log your hours accurately. You'll need them for the journey exam; backfilling years later is miserable.
  • Earn early certs (OSHA 30, forklift, first aid) — they read "serious" in your file.

Common Mistakes That Get Applicants Rejected

  • Applying to one program. Miss one cohort window and you wait months. Apply in parallel.
  • Skipping the math prep. The aptitude test filters more applicants than anything else. 2-3 weeks of timed algebra practice is your best ROI.
  • Leaving the interview vague. "I'm a hard worker" is a phrase, not an answer. Bring one specific story per question.
  • Ignoring the physical reality. Programs ask if you're ready for early starts, heat, cold, ladders, and lifting. Being honest and comfortable with that beats overselling a desk-job background.
  • Assuming trade school is required. It isn't (see below) — don't spend $10K-$15K on a head start you could get free through an apprenticeship.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I get a trade apprenticeship with no experience?

You don't need prior experience to enter a registered apprenticeship — that's the point. Apply through your state's Registered Apprenticeship system or a union hall, pass the aptitude test, and interview. Anything that makes you a more reliable hire — a driver's license, OSHA 10 safety certification, basic math refresher, and a willingness to start on a crew — dramatically improves your odds.

Do trade apprentices get paid?

Yes, from day one. Apprentices typically earn $15-$25 an hour in year one across trades, climbing to $21-$38/hr by year four. Union apprentices often start a step higher on published scale: IBEW electrician apprentices reach $25-$32/hr by year four, and the package usually includes health insurance, a pension, and paid classroom hours.

What is the aptitude test for trade apprenticeships?

Union apprenticeship programs (like IBEW and UA) use a standardized aptitude test covering algebra, reading comprehension, and mechanical reasoning. It's passable with a few weeks of focused prep — practice algebra fundamentals (fractions, percentages, systems of equations) and reading comprehension drills. Some halls publish practice tests or study guides.

How hard is it to get accepted into an apprenticeship?

Acceptance rates vary by trade, location, and demand, and competitive programs interview several applicants per seat. What improves your odds: applying to more than one program, having OSHA 10 or pre-apprenticeship training, showing reliability (attendance, no job-hopping), and interviewing well. Programs that are short on apprentices, like HVAC and electrical in many regions, accept much closer to everyone who passes the test.

Is a trade school required before an apprenticeship?

No. A registered apprenticeship is the primary path and pays you while you train. Trade school or community college programs are optional pre-education that can make you a stronger applicant and can sometimes shorten the classroom portion — but they cost money and time you don't spend while earning. Weigh the head start against the cost: many apprentices skip school entirely.

Do I need a high school diploma to get an apprenticeship?

Most registered apprenticeships require a high school diploma or GED, plus a passing aptitude test. Some programs will accept a completed GED-in-progress, and pre-apprenticeship programs exist specifically for students who haven't finished yet. Math coursework is the part that shows up most on the test, so a good algebra foundation or a refresher matters more than your GPA.

Your Next Move

The whole system fits on one action plan: decide your trade this week, apply to two or three registered programs in the same window, spend three weeks prepping algebra, and walk into the interview with two reliability stories ready. Trade school is optional; the process is not scary — it's just sequential. Go compare programs on the Trade Schools & Training hub, and if you're still deciding between electrician, plumber, HVAC, or welder, the 2-minute quiz will settle it.

Pro Tip: The #1 underused move is the pre-apprenticeship or helper entry. Walking into a non-union contractor's shop, asking to start as a helper, and then having them register your hours is how thousands of apprentices effectively skip the waitlist — and you go in already knowing the crew's tools and language.
