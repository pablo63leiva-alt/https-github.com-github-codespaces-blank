Apprentice Wages by Year: Real Pay for 6 Trades

Updated September 11, 2026 · 10 min read · Career Guide

Apprentice pay varies by trade, union status, and state — but it always climbs on a published schedule. Here's the year-by-year comparison across six trades, from first-year wages to journeyman pay, with the BLS medians for each.

Search "apprentice wages by year" and you'll find spreadsheets that contradict each other: one says $17 an hour, another says $35, a third claims six figures by year three. All can be true — because the trade, the union contract, and the state swing the number by thousands. Here's the whole picture across six trades, one year at a time.

The headline first: every registered apprenticeship pays you from week one. The U.S. Department of Labor reports an average starting wage around $17 an hour for registered apprentices, with scheduled raises each training year. By year four, apprentices in the trades below sit near 80-90% of journeyman scale, and licensed journeymen land between $45K and $100K+ depending on the trade. This guide compares them all with the same set of columns — year by year, union vs non-union — so you can see which path pays you most.

The Short Answer: You Get Paid More Every Year

The single biggest difference between trade training and a four-year degree: a degree bills you, an apprenticeship pays you. Year-1 apprentices start between $15 and $25 an hour across trades depending on market and union. By year 3-4, most sit between $17 and $38 an hour. The full picture of why apprenticeships beat college on cost — opportunity cost included — is in our is trade school worth it guide, and the earning mechanics on our Trades page.

What Each Trade Pays, Year by Year

Here's the nuance the table can't show: how wages move inside each trade, how union vs non-union changes the math, and which raises land hardest.

Electrician (IBEW)

First-year apprentice electricians earn between $15 and $22 an hour ($17-$22 on IBEW scale), climbing to $25-$32/hr on union scale by year 4. Licensed journey-level electricians land at $60K-$80K a year, and the BLS median is $61,590. The year-by-year union table plus state-by-state bands are in our electrician apprentice salary guide.

Plumber (UA)

First-year plumber apprentices earn $16-$22 an hour ($18-$22 on UA scale), reaching $22-$32/hr by year 4. Licensed journeymen earn $55K-$75K, about $59,880 at the BLS median, and master plumbers push well into $75K-$100K+. Full year-by-year numbers live in our plumber apprentice salary guide.

HVAC Technician (UA / non-union)

HVAC apprentices start at $15-$20/hr ($17-$20 on local union scale), reach $21-$28/hr by year 4, and licensed technicians earn $25-$33/hr non-union or $27-$35+/hr on union scale — a $57,310 BLS median. The EPA 608 certification and the full year-by-year table are in our HVAC apprentice salary guide.

Welder

Welders usually take a 6-12 month certificate path instead of a 4-year apprenticeship, so the year columns above are experience bands. Entry welders earn $16-$20/hr, and certified welders sit in the $45K-$70K range nationally — the BLS median is roughly $47,000. Specialties like pipe and underwater welding push top earners past $100K. Our is welding a good career guide breaks down the median-versus-hype math.

Carpenter (UBC)

Carpentry runs a 3-4 year apprenticeship. First-year carpenters earn $15-$19/hr, climbing toward $18-$26/hr by years 3-4, and experienced carpenters land in the $45K-$65K range — a $51,390 BLS median. Carpentry compounds fastest through specialties like formwork, finish, and commercial work.

Elevator Installer (IUEC)

The highest-paying apprentice path on this list — and the least known. IUEC apprentice installers start at $20-$25/hr on union scale, reach $29-$38/hr by year 4, and mechanics sit at a $100,080 BLS median, or $80K-$100K+ in practice. It's a niche, heavily unionized trade with a demanding 4-year apprenticeship and far fewer openings than the other trades here — most come through the National Elevator Industry Educational Program.

Union vs Non-Union: What Actually Changes

Union apprenticeships — IBEW for electricians, UA for plumbers and pipefitters, UBC for carpenters, IUEC for elevator installers — come with published scale rates, guaranteed raises, health coverage, a pension, and funded training. You pay zero tuition. Non-union shops vary more: some match union rates and benefits, others offer just the paycheck. The key difference isn't always the hourly rate — it's total compensation. A union apprentice at $18/hr with a health plan and pension is frequently ahead of a non-union apprentice at $21/hr with no benefits behind it. When you compare apprentice pay by year, ask what the whole package is worth. The Getting Started guide lists exactly which questions to ask during the application process.

The Biggest Raises Happen in Years 3-4

The step schedule isn't flat — the biggest hourly jumps land in years 3-4, when apprentices climb from roughly 50-60% of journeyman scale toward 80-90%. Look at the pattern: an IBEW electrician goes from $17-$22/hr in year 1 to $22-$28/hr by year 3, then $25-$32/hr in year 4. A UA plumber goes $18-$22 → $23-$28 → $25-$32 across the same years. Then the licensing jump — from year-4 scale to full journeyman pay — is the largest single raise of the entire career. That year-4 stretch is where the "stay with it" advice actually pays. Two trades with the same entry wage can be $8,000+/year apart by year 4 based on step speed: elevator installers and union electricians gain the fastest, so the low-year drudgery has the highest payoff floor.

Overtime and Certifications Move the Needle Faster

Step raises are guaranteed, but these levers add thousands on top:

  • Overtime. Construction work routinely pays time-and-a-half, and night, weekend, and call-out shifts hit double-time. A year-1 apprentice at ~$18/hr averaging 3-4 extra hours a week at time-and-a-half adds about $4,000-$5,500 a year — a raise nobody had to negotiate.
  • Getting licensed or certified. Passing the journeyman exam, the EPA 608, or an AWS welding cert is the single biggest raise of the career — it moves you from apprentice scale to full professional pay in one step.
  • Specialties. Industrial controls, commercial refrigeration, pipe welding, medical gas, and solar all pay premiums over general work. Specialized craftspeople regularly clear the top of the bands above.
  • Travel and out-of-town jobs. Union apprentices can chase per-diem, travel-bonus, and prevailing-wage jobs that pay above local scale.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much do apprentices make in 2026?

Year-1 apprentices typically earn $15-$25 an hour across trades, climbing to $21-$38/hr by year 4. First-year electricians start around $15-$22/hr, plumbers $16-$22/hr, and HVAC apprentices $15-$20/hr. By journeyman status, median pay lands at $61,590 for electricians, $59,880 for plumbers, and $57,310 for HVAC technicians — with top trades like elevator installers at a $100,080 BLS median.

Which trade pays apprentices the most by year 4?

Elevator installer apprentices (IUEC) top the list on union scale, with year 4 typically reaching $29-$38/hr before the jump to mechanic pay above $80K-$100K+. Electrician apprentices on IBEW scale reach $25-$32/hr by year 4, plumbing union scale hits $25-$32/hr, and union HVAC apprentices reach $24-$28/hr.

How much do apprentice wages go up each year?

Expect bumps of roughly $2-$6 an hour per step on union scales. The biggest single moves land in years 3-4, when apprentices climb from about 50-60% of journeyman scale toward 80-90%, and the jump to full journeyman pay at licensing is the largest of the career.

Do apprentices get paid while they train?

Yes, from day one. Apprentices start at roughly 40-50% of journeyman scale with scheduled raises every 6-12 months. Union programs cost $0 in tuition while paying you, so the same 4-5 years that send a college graduate into debt leave you at a full professional wage with zero student loans.
