Carpenter Apprentice Salary: The Year-by-Year Pay Breakdown

Updated September 14, 2026 · 8 min read · Career Guide

Carpenter apprentices earn real wages from day one, with scheduled raises every year of a 3-4 year program. Here's exactly what a first-year carpenter apprentice makes in 2026, how pay climbs year by year, what separates union (UBC local) from non-union, and the path from apprentice to journey-level carpenter.

Search "carpenter apprentice salary" and you'll get answers ranging from $14 an hour to $30 — and most of them are true, depending on where you train, whether you go union, and which state you're in. Carpentry is the most accessible of the building trades, with a clear 3-4 year apprenticeship from helper to journey-level carpenter. Here's the real, year-by-year version.

The headline first: carpenter apprentices earn while they learn. You start at roughly 40-50% of a journeyman wage and get bumps on a published schedule. Most registered carpentry apprenticeships run 3-4 years, log around 4,800 on-the-job hours on the union (UBC) track, and finish at journey-level carpenter pay of $44K-$60K a year — the Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a median of $51,390. Foremen, lead carpenters, and specialty carpenters push well past that. This guide walks you through every step, one year at a time.

The Short Answer: You Get Paid From Day One

The single biggest difference between carpentry training and a four-year degree: a degree bills you, an apprenticeship pays you. In 2026, first-year carpenter apprentices typically earn between $15 and $19 an hour depending on market and union. By year four, most apprentices sit between $18 and $26 an hour — near 80-90% of journey scale. Licensed journey-level carpenters land in the $44K-$60K range nationally, and carpenters specializing in commercial formwork, millwork, and finish carpentry regularly push past that. Union (UBC Local) vs Non-Union: What Actually Changes

Union carpentry apprenticeships — through UBC (United Brotherhood of Carpenters) locals — come with published scale rates, guaranteed step raises, pension and health benefits, and training funded through the apprenticeship program. You pay zero tuition. Non-union shops vary more: some match union rates and benefits, others offer just the paycheck. The key difference in practice isn't always the hourly rate — it's the benefits package. A UBC apprentice at $16/hr with full health coverage, a pension contribution, and funded classroom hours is frequently ahead of a non-union apprentice at $17/hr with no benefits behind it. It also changes the year-by-year math in your favor. A year-1 apprentice at $16-$19/hr grosses roughly $32K-$38K on a standard 40-hour week — real money, and more importantly money you didn't borrow. Run a rough four-year comparison: a four-year degree frequently costs over $100K in tuition, fees, and forgone earnings. A 3-4 year carpentry apprenticeship pays you from week one, and adding up the annual columns above gives roughly $150K-$185K in cumulative take-home pay while you earn toward journey status — then drops you at year four sitting near 80-90% of journey scale instead of entering the entry-level job market. The Apprentice to Journeyman Carpentry Path

Reaching journey status is the biggest single raise of your career — but it's not the ceiling. Here's what comes after:

  • Journeyman carpenter. You've completed the apprenticeship (3-4 years, ~4,800 OJT hours on the UBC track), met the state or union credential requirements, and can work independently. Typical pay: $44K-$60K, about $51,390 at the BLS median. You can read blueprints, do layout, and run framing, finish, and commercial work without supervision.
  • Lead carpenter / foreman. Typically offered after 1-3 years of journey experience. Lead carpenters run crews and coordinate daily work on-site, moving from $44K-$60K into the $60K-$80K+ range.
  • Specializations. Commercial formwork, finish and millwork, cabinetry, trim, and scaffold installation all carry premium rates over general residential work — sometimes 15-30% higher.

Every one of these steps builds on the previous one — and every one of them pays you while you earn it. The full certification and licensing roadmap lives on our Resources page.

What Boosts a Carpenter Apprentice's Pay the Fastest

Your step raises are guaranteed, but these levers move the needle much faster:

  • Overtime. Construction work routinely pays time-and-a-half, and rush commercial jobs regularly hit it as well. A few overtime weeks a month quietly adds thousands to the table above.
  • Getting your journey credential. Completing the apprenticeship and earning journey status is the single biggest raise of your career — it takes you from the year-4 scale to full journey pay in one step.
  • Specialties. Formwork, finish, millwork, cabinetry, and trim all pay premiums over general framing work.
  • Travel and out-of-town work. Union carpenters can chase per-diem, travel-bonus, and prevailing-wage jobs that pay above local scale, especially on large commercial or industrial projects.
  • The foreman track (later). Running a crew moves you from journey scale to the $60K-$80K+ range — and stops the hourly ceiling from applying entirely at larger firms.

None of these require a four-year degree. The step-by-step roadmap for each one is in our Getting Started guide.

How Carpentry Pay Stacks Up Against the Other Trades

Carpenters are the backbone of every construction site — and the pay reflects that steady demand, even if it's not the flashiest number on the board. For context on where the $44K-$60K journey band sits, here's how it compares to the other apprenticeship trades we've broken down year by year:

  • Electrician: $15-$32/hr across apprentice years, with journey-level pay of $60K-$80K and a $61,590 BLS median — the highest earners of the licensed trades. See the electrician apprentice salary breakdown.
  • Plumber: $16-$32/hr across apprentice years, with journeyman pay of $56K-$74K and a $59,880 BLS median. Full year-by-year numbers are in the plumber apprentice salary guide.
  • HVAC technician: $15-$30/hr across apprentice years, with certified techs at $50K-$70K and a $57,310 BLS median. See the HVAC apprentice salary breakdown.
  • All six trades side by side: The full cross-trade comparison — including welders and elevator installers — lives in our apprentice wages by year guide.

The pattern: carpentry trades a slightly lower ceiling for the widest, most forgiving entry door of any building trade. You don't need to pass an aptitude-heavy licensing exam — you need to show up, learn quickly, and prove you can keep a crew's pace.

Job Outlook: Why Carpentry Demand Stays Constant

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 4% growth for carpenters through 2033, with roughly 92,500 openings each year from new positions and replacement needs. That's steady, reliable demand — and the key drivers are structural:

  • Every building needs framing, trim, and finish — nothing gets occupied without a carpenter
  • New residential and commercial construction generates constant demand
  • Remodels and renovations keep existing carpenters busy in every market
  • Aging infrastructure and commercial retrofits add to the workload
  • Formwork and concrete systems rely on carpenters on every large job

Carpentry is one of the most accessible routes into construction because the barrier to entry is a willingness to work and learn — not a license or a test. That accessibility is why carpentry ranks near the top of our highest paying jobs without a degree list.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a carpenter apprentice make in 2026?

Typically $15-$26 an hour depending on year, region, and union vs non-union. First-year carpenter apprentices start around $15-$19/hr, year-4 apprentices reach $18-$26/hr, and licensed journey-level carpenters land in the $44K-$60K band — a BLS median of $51,390 a year — before overtime or specialty work.

How long does a carpenter apprenticeship take?

Most registered carpentry apprenticeships run 3-4 years, combining on-the-job training with classroom instruction. The union (UBC) program is typically 4 years and roughly 4,800 on-the-job hours before you reach journey status. Completion leads straight to full journeyman pay — no college debt involved.

Do carpenter apprentices get paid during training?

Yes. Apprentices earn a wage from day one, starting at roughly 40-50% of journeyman scale with scheduled raises every 6-12 months. Union apprenticeship programs (through UBC locals) cost $0 in tuition while paying you. By the time you reach journey status, you are earning a full professional wage with zero student debt.

What is the difference between a carpenter apprentice and a journeyman?

An apprentice is a trainee working under supervision while earning a wage and completing a 3-4 year registered program. A journeyman carpenter has finished the apprenticeship (and often a state or union credential), can read blueprints and layout, and works independently on framing, finish, and commercial work — at the full wage scale, typically $44K-$60K a year before overtime.

What is the job outlook for carpenters?

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 4% growth for carpenters through 2033, with roughly 92,500 openings each year from growth and replacement needs. New construction, remodels, and aging infrastructure keep demand steady, and carpentry is one of the most accessible entry points among the construction trades.
