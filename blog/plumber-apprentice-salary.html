Plumber Apprentice Salary: The Year-by-Year Pay Breakdown

Updated September 11, 2026 · 8 min read · Career Guide

Plumber apprentices earn real wages from day one, with scheduled raises every year of a 4-5 year program. Here's exactly what a first-year plumber apprentice makes in 2026, how pay climbs year by year, what separates union (UA local) from non-union, and the licensing path from apprentice to journeyman to master plumber.

Search "plumber apprentice salary" and you'll find numbers ranging from $16 an hour to $35 — and all of them can be true, depending on where you train, whether you go union, and which state you're in. The plumbing trade has one of the most structured apprenticeship paths in the skilled trades, with a clear 4-5 year progression from apprentice to journeyman. Here's the real, year-by-year version.

The headline first: plumber apprentices earn while they learn. You start at roughly 40-50% of a journeyman wage and get bumps on a published schedule. Most registered plumbing apprenticeships run 4-5 years, log around 8,000 on-the-job hours, and finish at journeyman plumber pay of $55K-$75K a year — the Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a median of $59,880. Master plumbers push well past that, often into the $75K-$100K+ range. This guide walks you through every step, one year at a time.

The Short Answer: You Get Paid From Day One

The single biggest difference between plumbing training and a four-year degree: a degree bills you, an apprenticeship pays you. In 2026, first-year plumber apprentices typically earn between $16 and $22 an hour depending on market and union. By year four, most apprentices sit between $22 and $32 an hour — near 80-90% of journeyman scale. Licensed journeyman plumbers land in the $55K-$75K range nationally, and plumbers specializing in commercial, industrial pipefitting, or gas fitting regularly push past that. Union (UA Local) vs Non-Union: What Actually Changes

Union plumbing apprenticeships — through UA (United Association) locals — come with published scale rates, guaranteed step raises, pension and health benefits, and training funded through the apprenticeship program. You pay zero tuition. Non-union shops vary more: some match union rates and benefits, others offer just the paycheck. The key difference in practice isn't always the hourly rate — it's the benefits package. A UA apprentice at $18/hr with full health coverage, a pension contribution, and funded classroom hours is frequently ahead of a non-union apprentice at $21/hr with no benefits behind it. It also changes the year-by-year math in your favor. A year-1 apprentice at $18-$22/hr grosses roughly $36K-$44K on a standard 40-hour week — real money, and more importantly money you didn't borrow. Run a rough four-year comparison: a four-year degree frequently costs over $100K in tuition, fees, and forgone earnings. A 4-5 year plumbing apprenticeship pays you from week one, and adding up the annual columns above gives roughly $170K-$215K in cumulative take-home pay while you earn toward the license — then drops you at year four sitting near 80-90% of journeyman scale instead of entering the entry-level job market. The Journeyman to Master Plumber Path

Reaching journeyman status is the biggest single raise of your career — but it's not the ceiling. Here's what comes after:

  • Journeyman plumber. You've completed the apprenticeship (4-5 years, ~8,000 hours), passed the state exam, and can work independently. Median pay: $59,880 (BLS 2024). You can work for any employer and pull off journeyman-level work without supervision.
  • Master plumber. Typically requires 2-5 additional years of journeyman experience (varies by state), then passing a more advanced exam covering codes, business, and complex systems. Master plumbers can pull permits, run their own business, and hire apprentices. Earning potential: $75,000-$100,000+. Many master plumbers own their own shops, where the income ceiling is essentially unlimited.
  • Specializations. Medical gas piping, fire sprinkler systems, gas fitting, backflow prevention, and industrial pipefitting all carry premium certifications that push earnings above general plumbing work.

Every one of these steps builds on the previous one — and every one of them pays you while you earn it. The full certification and licensing roadmap lives on our Resources page.

What Boosts a Plumber Apprentice's Pay the Fastest

Your step raises are guaranteed, but these levers move the needle much faster:

  • Overtime. Plumbing work routinely pays time-and-a-half, and emergency calls (burst pipes, sewer backups) often hit double-time. A few overtime weeks a month quietly adds thousands to the table above.
  • Getting your journeyman license. Passing the exam is the single biggest raise of your career — it takes you from the year-4 scale to full journeyman pay (the $56K-$74K row) in one step.
  • Specialties. Medical gas, fire sprinkler, backflow prevention, and industrial pipefitting all carry premium certifications that pay more than general residential work.
  • Travel and out-of-town work. Union apprentices can chase per-diem and travel-bonus jobs that pay above local scale, especially on large commercial or industrial projects.
  • The master license (later). A master plumber license lets you pull permits, run your own business, and hire apprentices — that's when the hourly ceiling stops applying entirely.

None of these require a four-year degree. The step-by-step roadmap for each one is in our Getting Started guide.

Job Outlook: Why Plumbing Is Recession-Resistant

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 4% growth for plumbers and pipefitters through 2033, with roughly 45,500 openings each year from new positions and replacement needs. That's steady, reliable demand — and the key drivers are structural:

  • Every building needs working water supply and sewer systems
  • Aging infrastructure across the U.S. requires replacement and repair
  • New construction generates constant demand for plumbing installation
  • Water efficiency regulations drive upgrades and retrofits
  • Gas line work for residential and commercial applications adds to the workload

Plumbing is one of the most recession-resistant trades because it deals with essential services. Pipes burst, sewers clog, and water heaters fail regardless of what the economy is doing. That demand stability is one of the biggest reasons plumbing ranks in the top five on our highest paying jobs without a degree list.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a plumber apprentice make in 2026?

Typically $16-$32 an hour depending on year, region, and union vs non-union. First-year plumber apprentices start around $16-$22/hr, year-4 apprentices reach $22-$32/hr, and licensed journeyman plumbers land at a median of $59,880 a year — roughly $28-$32/hr — before overtime or master plumber earnings.

How long does it take to become a licensed plumber?

Most registered plumbing apprenticeships run 4-5 years, combining on-the-job training with classroom instruction. You log roughly 8,000 on-the-job hours before sitting for the journeyman plumber licensing exam. After that, many states require 2-5 additional years of experience before you can test for a master plumber license.

Do plumber apprentices get paid during training?

Yes. Apprentices earn a wage from day one, starting at roughly 40-50% of journeyman scale with scheduled raises every 6-12 months. Union apprenticeship programs (through UA locals) cost $0 in tuition while paying you. By the time you reach journeyman status, you are earning a full professional wage with zero student debt.

What is the difference between a journeyman and master plumber?

A journeyman plumber has completed the apprenticeship, passed the licensing exam, and can work independently under a master plumber's license. A master plumber has typically 2-5 additional years of journeyman experience, has passed a more advanced exam, and can pull permits, run their own business, and hire apprentices. Master plumbers earn significantly more — often $75,000-$100,000+.

What is the job outlook for plumbers?

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 4% growth for plumbers and pipefitters through 2033, with roughly 45,500 openings each year from growth and replacement needs. Aging infrastructure, new construction, and water efficiency regulations keep demand steady. Plumbing is one of the most recession-resistant trades because every building needs working water and sewer systems.
